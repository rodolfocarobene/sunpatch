How many hours of sun does an apartment get in winter?

By Rodolfo Carobene, updated 20 November 2026

It depends on three things: how long the sun is up, which way your windows face, and what stands in front of them. Here they are in turn, with Milan as the example.

Winter days are short and the sun stays low

At 45.5° N, about the latitude of Milan, the sun is up for roughly 8 hours 45 minutes on 21 December. On 21 June it is up for roughly 15 hours 40 minutes. That daylight is the ceiling. A window cannot get more direct sun than the hours the sun is above the horizon.

The sun is also low. At solar noon, around 12:20 by the clock in Milan, it reaches only about 21° above the horizon on 21 December. On 21 June it climbs to about 68° (around 13:25 summer time). The rule of thumb is: noon height = 90° minus the latitude, plus or minus 23.4° for the season.

A worked example: why a south-facing flat gets winter sun deep into the room

Take a south-facing window with its sill 0.9 m above the floor and its top at 2.2 m. At noon the sun is straight in front of it. A beam that enters at height h lands on the floor at a distance of h divided by tan(sun height) from the window.

  • 21 December, sun at 21°: the patch starts 2.3 m from the window and ends 5.7 m from it.
  • 21 June, sun at 68°: the patch runs from 0.4 m to 0.9 m. Almost all of it stays right by the window.

So the same window gives a long, deep patch in winter and a short strip in summer. These figures assume the sun is straight in front of a thin wall. Thick walls, deep window reveals and balconies trim the patch.

What cuts the hours: orientation and neighbours

In Milan on 21 December the sun rises in the south-east and sets in the south-west, so a south-facing window can see it almost all day. An east window gets about four hours in the morning, a west window about four in the afternoon, and a north window gets none. The guide to south, east, west and north goes through each side.

Neighbours matter just as much. A taller building across the street can hide the low winter sun from the lower floors, while the same street is bright in June. See how to check whether the building across the street blocks your sun.

All of this counts hours with a clear sky. Cloud and fog take some of them away, so read the result as the best case.

Check your own flat

In Sunpatch you pick the building on the map, sketch your flat and its windows, set the date to 21 December and switch on the sun map. The floor is coloured by hours of direct sun, and a click on any spot gives the times.

Try the Milan example Open the app

What Sunpatch models and what it does not. It counts direct sun only: the beam through each window, with shadows from your building and its neighbours. Building heights come from OpenStreetMap and can be wrong. Roofs are flat and there is no terrain yet, so distant hills are ignored. Diffuse light is a separate estimate and is never mixed into the sun hours. Times follow your browser's time zone. Treat the result as a good estimate, not a survey.
