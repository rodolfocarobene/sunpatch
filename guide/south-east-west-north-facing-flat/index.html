South, east, west or north facing: which flat gets the most sun?

By Rodolfo Carobene, updated 20 November 2026

In the northern hemisphere a south-facing flat gets the most direct sun over the year, and the most in winter. But the best side depends on what you want, and on what stands in front of the windows. Here is how each side behaves, using Milan (45.5° N) as the example.

The sun path in Milan

  • 21 December: the sun rises in the south-east (azimuth about 124°) and sets in the south-west (about 237°). At noon it is only 21° high.
  • 21 June: it rises in the north-east (about 55°) and sets in the north-west (about 306°). At noon it is 68° high.
  • Around 21 March and 23 September: it rises almost due east and sets almost due west.

A window gets direct sun only while the sun is on its side of the wall. That is the whole story, and it explains each orientation.

Side by side

  • South. In winter the sun is in front of the window for nearly the whole day, about 8.5 hours on 21 December, and low enough to reach deep into the room. In summer it is high, so the patch stays near the window and a balcony or an awning easily shades it. This is the side most people want.
  • East. Morning sun all year round, from sunrise to about noon. In December that is about four hours, with a low sun. The room is cooler in the afternoon.
  • West. The mirror image: afternoon and evening sun, about four hours in December. The sun arrives when the day is already warm, so west rooms are usually the hardest to keep cool in summer.
  • North. No direct sun in winter at this latitude, because the sun never gets north of the east-west line. In June the sun reaches north windows for up to about three and a half hours at each end of the day, at a low angle. Light in a north room is steady, but the room can feel dark and cold in winter.

In the southern hemisphere the picture flips: north is the sunny side.

Orientation is a first guess, not the answer

Neighbours can change the ranking. A south-facing flat on a low floor behind a tall building gets less winter sun than a north-east flat on a top floor with an open view. Height, balconies above you, trees and the width of the street all count. The next guide covers how to check the building across the street. See it for your own flat

In Sunpatch you draw the windows on each side of your flat and slide through the day. The patches of sun show which room is lit and when. Set the date to 21 December and then to 21 June to see both extremes. The Milan example is a good place to start.

What Sunpatch models and what it does not. It counts direct sun only, through each window you draw, with shadows from your building and the neighbours on the map. Heights come from OpenStreetMap and can be missing or wrong. Roofs are flat, trees are rough, and terrain is not modelled yet. Shaded rooms look dark because diffuse light is only a separate estimate. Times follow your browser's time zone.
